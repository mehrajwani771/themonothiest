The Science of Learning

How the Human Mind Acquires, Retains and Applies Knowledge
Published: 11 November 2026   |   Category: Education, Cognitive Psychology & Learning

Why do some students remember what they learn for years, while others forget an entire chapter shortly after an examination? Why does spending more time studying not necessarily produce better results? And why do certain learning methods feel productive even when they contribute very little to long-term understanding?

These questions lie at the heart of the science of learning.

For decades, cognitive psychologists, neuroscientists and educational researchers have investigated how human beings acquire information, construct understanding, retain knowledge and apply what they have learned to unfamiliar situations.

Their findings challenge many traditional assumptions about studying. Reading the same chapter repeatedly, highlighting entire pages and copying notes may create a sense of familiarity, but familiarity alone is not evidence of genuine understanding.

Learning involves much more than exposure to information. It requires the formation of meaningful connections, retrieval of previously acquired knowledge, correction of errors and the ability to use information independently.

Research has identified several particularly effective learning strategies, including retrieval practice, spaced practice, interleaving and elaboration. However, these techniques do not operate in isolation. Their effectiveness depends on prior knowledge, the nature of the subject, the learner's level of understanding, feedback and the conditions under which learning takes place.

The central objective of education should not be to help students merely remember information long enough to pass an examination. It should be to develop knowledge that remains accessible, understanding that can withstand questioning, and intellectual abilities that can be applied beyond the classroom.

This article examines the scientific foundations of learning, the strategies supported by research, the habits that often create an illusion of mastery, and the practical methods through which students and teachers can build a more effective learning system.

Part I: Understanding How Learning Happens

1. What Does It Actually Mean to Learn?

Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge, understanding or the ability to perform a task as a result of experience, instruction or practice.

However, learning should not be confused with immediate performance. A student may answer a question correctly because the answer was recently demonstrated, yet fail to reproduce the same answer independently several days later.

Genuine learning becomes evident when knowledge can be retrieved, explained and applied beyond the immediate learning situation.

A useful way to understand the development of knowledge is through six interconnected intellectual activities:

  1. Remembering: Recalling facts, definitions and information.
  2. Understanding: Explaining ideas in one's own words.
  3. Applying: Using acquired knowledge to solve problems.
  4. Analysing: Examining relationships, patterns and assumptions.
  5. Evaluating: Judging arguments and evidence.
  6. Creating: Producing new ideas or solutions using existing knowledge.

These activities are not necessarily a rigid sequence. They interact with one another, and different learning objectives require different combinations of them.

2. Working Memory and Long-Term Memory

The human mind processes information through several interacting memory systems. Two particularly important concepts in educational psychology are working memory and long-term memory.

Working Memory: The limited-capacity system that temporarily maintains and manipulates information while a person performs a mental task.
Long-Term Memory: The relatively durable system through which knowledge, experiences and skills can be retained over extended periods.

When a student solves a mathematical equation, working memory is involved in holding numbers, operations and intermediate results in mind.

When the student has practised the relevant mathematical procedures sufficiently, some of the required knowledge becomes readily accessible from long-term memory. This reduces the mental effort required for familiar operations.

One important educational implication is that students should not be overwhelmed with too much unfamiliar information at once.

Teachers can support learning by breaking complex explanations into manageable sections, introducing prerequisite knowledge and gradually increasing the complexity of tasks.

3. Prior Knowledge and the Construction of Understanding

Learning does not begin with an empty mind. Every learner approaches new information with existing knowledge, experiences, assumptions and expectations.

New information becomes easier to understand when students can connect it to concepts they already know.

For example, a student who understands basic addition may find multiplication easier when it is introduced as repeated addition.

However, prior knowledge can also create difficulties. If students have developed incorrect beliefs, they may interpret new information in ways that reinforce existing misconceptions.

Teachers should therefore identify what students already know before introducing complex concepts.

Part II: Evidence-Based Learning Strategies

4. Retrieval Practice

Retrieval practice is the process of recalling information from memory without continuously referring to the original source.

Instead of repeatedly reading a chapter, students attempt to reconstruct its contents independently. They may answer questions, use flashcards, write from memory or explain a concept without consulting their notes.

Research indicates that retrieval practice can improve long-term retention and make previously learned information more accessible.

For example, after studying the human digestive system, a student closes the textbook and attempts to:

  • Name the major organs involved in digestion.
  • Explain the function of the stomach.
  • Describe how nutrients are absorbed.
  • Draw the digestive system from memory.

The student then checks the textbook and corrects missing or inaccurate information.

Retrieval is not simply a test of existing knowledge. It is also an opportunity to strengthen learning and identify weaknesses.

However, retrieval should be appropriately challenging. When students cannot recall anything, teachers can provide hints, partial answers or additional instruction.

Practical principle: Read a section, close the book, recall what you have learned, check your understanding and correct your mistakes.

5. Spaced Practice and Spaced Retrieval

Spaced practice involves distributing learning sessions across multiple days or weeks rather than concentrating all study into a single session.

Cramming may improve short-term performance, but it often produces weaker long-term retention than appropriately distributed study.

Spaced retrieval combines two useful techniques: recalling information and repeating that retrieval after intervals of time.

For example, a student learning a chapter might revisit it after two days, one week and two weeks. These intervals are practical examples, not universal scientific prescriptions.

The appropriate schedule depends on the difficulty of the material, the learner's existing knowledge, retrieval success and how long the information needs to be retained.

A meta-analysis by Latimier, Peyre and Ramus (2021), examining 29 studies, found that spaced retrieval produced better retention than massed retrieval. It also found no significant general advantage for expanding intervals over uniform intervals.

The practical lesson is simple: distribute revision across time and adjust the schedule according to learning needs.

6. Interleaving

Interleaving involves mixing related topics or problem types rather than practising only one type of question repeatedly.

Consider a mathematics student who has studied fractions, decimals and percentages.

In blocked practice, the student might solve thirty fraction problems followed by thirty decimal problems.

In interleaved practice, different problem types are mixed together.

The learner must identify what kind of problem is being presented before selecting an appropriate solution method.

This is particularly useful when students need to distinguish between similar concepts, formulas or procedures.

However, beginners should first receive sufficient explanation and focused practice. Interleaving unfamiliar material too early can create unnecessary cognitive difficulty.

A sensible progression is:

  1. Understand the concept.
  2. Practise the concept independently.
  3. Mix it with previously learned concepts.
  4. Apply it to unfamiliar problems.

7. Elaboration and Self-Explanation

Elaboration involves connecting new information with existing knowledge by asking questions such as:

  • Why is this true?
  • How does this work?
  • What evidence supports this statement?
  • How is this connected to what I already know?

Self-explanation is related but distinct. It involves explaining the reasoning behind a concept or the steps used to solve a problem.

For example, after learning about photosynthesis, a student might ask why plants require sunlight and then explain how light energy contributes to the production of glucose.

This process can expose gaps in understanding that may remain hidden during passive reading.

8. Pretesting

Pretesting means attempting to answer questions before studying the material in detail.

Its purpose is not to assess final knowledge. Instead, it prepares the learner to pay attention to important information during subsequent instruction.

Before studying a chapter about the human circulatory system, a student might attempt to answer:

  • What is the function of the heart?
  • Why does blood circulate throughout the body?
  • What is the difference between arteries and veins?

Even incorrect attempts may help direct attention towards relevant information.

However, pretesting should be followed by instruction and corrective feedback.

9. Dual Coding and Concrete Examples

Dual coding involves presenting information through complementary verbal and visual representations.

For example, a written explanation of the water cycle becomes easier to understand when accompanied by a correctly labelled diagram showing evaporation, condensation and precipitation.

Visual representations can include:

  • Scientific diagrams.
  • Concept maps.
  • Flowcharts.
  • Timelines.
  • Geographical maps.
  • Graphs and labelled illustrations.

Concrete examples also help students connect abstract ideas with familiar experiences.

For instance, explaining the concept of an algorithm through the everyday process of preparing tea can make an abstract computational concept more accessible.

Visuals should be relevant, clearly labelled and connected to the explanation. Decorative images and excessive information can create additional cognitive load rather than improve learning.

10. Feedback and Error Correction

Learning is not complete when a student discovers that an answer is wrong. The student must understand why it is wrong and how to improve it.

Effective feedback identifies:

  1. What the learner did correctly.
  2. Where the mistake occurred.
  3. Why the mistake happened.
  4. How the correct approach should be applied.

Consider a student who incorrectly calculates the area of a rectangle by adding its length and breadth.

Simply marking the answer wrong provides limited guidance. Explaining that area measures the space enclosed by the rectangle and is calculated by multiplying length by breadth provides a more meaningful correction.

Students should maintain an error log to identify recurring mistakes and revisit them during subsequent study sessions.

Part III: The Psychology of Learning

11. The Illusion of Mastery

Many students confuse familiarity with understanding.

Reading the same chapter repeatedly may make its contents feel familiar. Highlighting important sentences may create a sense of productivity. Watching an educational video may produce the impression that a concept has been fully understood.

But these feelings do not necessarily indicate that knowledge can be retrieved or applied independently.

Study activity What it feels like Potential limitation
Repeated reading Familiarity Recognition without independent recall
Highlighting Productivity Attention without sufficient retrieval
Copying notes Completion Little meaningful processing if done mechanically
Watching videos Understanding Insufficient independent application
Cramming Immediate confidence Weak long-term retention
